So let's say you already boiled it. Is it over?

Not entirely. There is a backup route.

When myrosinase is gone, glucoraphanin travels down undigested, all the way to your large intestine. And there, some of your gut bacteria, particularly certain Bacteroides species, carry enzyme activity similar to myrosinase. Your bacteria end up doing the job the plant enzyme was supposed to do.

But this route is far less efficient, and far more variable. With the active plant enzyme, roughly a third of the glucoraphanin gets converted. When the enzyme has been destroyed, that number drops substantially, and it swings enormously from person to person.

The source of that difference is your own gut flora. Two people eating the exact same plate can deliver very different signals to their liver.

The timing changes too. With raw or lightly steamed broccoli, sulforaphane peaks in your blood within about one to three hours. With boiled broccoli, you have to wait for bacterial conversion, so the peak drifts out to around six hours, and the peak itself is lower.

Sulforaphane is absorbed through the cells of your small intestine, enters the portal circulation, and is carried straight to your liver.

This is where it gets interesting.

But first, let's kill a myth. Your liver does not need a detox. Your liver is the detox. The real question is what speeds that system up.

Your liver processes a foreign compound in two phases.

Phase one. The cytochrome P four fifty enzyme family goes to work and attaches a chemical handle to the molecule. But there is a catch that almost nobody mentions. The output of phase one is often more reactive than the input was. A harmless compound can be turned into an intermediate that is capable of binding to your DNA.

Phase two. Conjugation. Glutathione, sulfate, or glucuronic acid gets attached to that reactive intermediate. The molecule becomes water soluble, and it leaves your body through bile or urine.

So your actual safety lives in the balance between phase one and phase two. If phase two falls behind, reactive intermediates start accumulating inside the cell.

Sulforaphane intervenes at exactly that balance point. And the way it does it is one of the most elegant mechanisms in all of cell biology.

Inside the cytoplasm of your liver cell, there is a protein called Nerf two. Nerf two is a transcription factor. A switch that binds to DNA and turns genes on. But under normal conditions, it never gets the chance. A guardian protein called Keep one grabs hold of it and continuously ships it off to the proteasome, the cell's shredder. The lifespan of Nerf two is measured in minutes.

Now, Keep one is covered in highly reactive, sulfur containing cysteine residues. They work like sensors.

And sulforaphane is also a sulfur containing, electron hungry molecule. When it enters the liver cell, it chemically bonds to those critical cysteine sites on Keep one. That bond changes the three dimensional shape of Keep one, and its grip loosens.

Nerf two is released.

Free Nerf two moves into the nucleus, binds to specific DNA sequences called antioxidant response elements, and switches on an entire battery of genes all at once.

Quinone reductase and glutathione S transferase, both phase two conjugation enzymes. Heme oxygenase one, which protects the cell under stress. And glutamate cysteine ligase, the rate limiting enzyme for glutathione production, which means the cell starts manufacturing more of its own master antioxidant.

And now, the single most important sentence in this video.

Sulforaphane is not an antioxidant.

It does not get consumed neutralizing a radical the way vitamin C does. It is a signaling molecule that switches on your cell's own defense genes. It does not put out the fire. It calls the fire department.

Look at the difference. Direct antioxidants work stoichiometrically. One molecule neutralizes one radical, and then it is spent. The effect lasts hours. Nerf two activation is catalytic. Once gene expression has been triggered, the effect lasts for days. Long after sulforaphane has been cleared from your blood, the enzymes it ordered are still working.

That is why this is about eating broccoli regularly, not occasionally.
