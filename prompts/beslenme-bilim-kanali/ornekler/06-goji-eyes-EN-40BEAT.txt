There is a piece of your brain that sits outside your skull, and you are using it right now.

The retina is not attached to the brain. It is brain tissue, carried out to the front of your head.

It grows outward while you are still an embryo, and it stays nerve tissue for the rest of your life.

Near its centre sits a spot about one and a half millimetres across. That spot is called the macula.

Almost all of your detailed vision happens inside it. Every word you are reading lands there.

Which makes it the most light exposed tissue in your body, hour after hour, for decades.

It is also oxygen rich and full of fragile fats. Light, oxygen and fat together is how damage begins.

So your body installs a filter, a layer of pigment laid directly over the cells that matter most.

It works like an internal sunshade, and it is the reason a healthy macula looks faintly yellow.

Three molecules build it: lutein, zeaxanthin and meso zeaxanthin. Together they are macular pigment.

They absorb blue light before it reaches the photoreceptors, and they quench the oxygen that burns them.

And your body cannot make a single one of them. Every molecule of that filter arrived from food.

Which brings us to goji, the richest dietary source of zeaxanthin that we know of.

But the amount is not what makes goji unusual. What makes it unusual is the form it arrives in.

In goji the molecule already carries two fatty acid chains. It is bonded to fat before you ever eat it.

In most plants zeaxanthin arrives bare and unprotected. Here it arrives already wrapped in fat.

That matters, because carotenoids cannot cross your gut wall alone. They need micelles, and micelles need fat.

We said it with avocado. We said it again with tomato. This is the third time. The fat is the vehicle.

Packed inside a micelle, the molecule slips through the intestinal wall and into the body.

From there it rides a chylomicron into the lymphatic system rather than straight into the blood.

It joins the bloodstream further upstream, travelling among red cells like any other cargo.

Then it is handed off, transferred out of the chylomicron and into your lipoproteins.

And here the story splits from the last one, because zeaxanthin travels mostly in HDL.

Lycopene rode LDL into the artery wall. This molecule turns the other way, upward, toward the head.

The retinal circulation carries it to the back of the eye, and the delivery is not accidental.

There is a protein in retinal tissue whose job is to recognise this exact molecule.

It binds zeaxanthin selectively and draws it inward, and a second protein holds it in place.

Molecule by molecule the filter is rebuilt. Your eye has a receiving station for something it cannot make.

So does eating goji change anything measurable? Unusually, yes, because this filter can be read directly.

A trial compared twenty eight grams of goji berries against a lutein and zeaxanthin supplement.

Twenty seven adults, aged forty five to sixty five, split into two groups, followed for ninety days.

The berry group's macular pigment rose significantly. The supplement group did not change at all.

Whole food won and the isolated molecule did nothing. That is the question we ended the tomato video on.

Maybe it is the ester form, maybe the fat around it, maybe the company the molecule keeps inside the fruit.

We do not know. And this was a pilot, twenty seven people, unblinded, measuring a filter rather than sight.

So, four rules. One. About twenty eight grams a day, a small handful. That is the dose that was studied.

Two. Eat them with fat. Without fat there is no micelle, and without a micelle there is no delivery.

Three. Whole berries rather than an extract, because the whole fruit is what actually worked here.

Four. Think in months, not weeks. The change was measured after ninety days, not after a fortnight.
